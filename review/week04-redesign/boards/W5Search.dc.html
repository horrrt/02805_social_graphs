A Marvel search engine in 20 lines

Counting shared words beats chance, but it loves short pages: a 255-word page beats Storm's 9,831 words for "weather-controlling mutant from Kenya".

What we asked

Can a Bag-of-Words search find the right Marvel page from a short description?

Rarely first: the right page comes first for 1 of 11 queries and in the top five for 6. Short pages win most misses.

What we did

We turned every page and every query into a A page or a query as a list of word counts, with the word order thrown away.: a count of each word, with the order thrown away. A word is a run of letters and digits in any alphabet, lowercased, and a hyphen splits words, so Spider-Man counts as spider and man. The 303 pages and their 28,062 different words form a A table with one row per page, one column per word and the counts inside. that is 97.2% empty. The most common word, the, makes up 6.1% of all 740,340 words: a handful of words dominate every page, as Zipf's law says.

For each query we ranked all 303 pages by How close two count vectors point: their dot product divided by both their lengths, from 0 (no shared word) to 1 (the same proportions). and looked where the page we meant came. We wrote 12 queries, each with a target page. The brief's own example, "Norse god of thunder", has no target: the snapshot holds no page for Thor, so it stays out of the hit rates. Then we ran every query again with Very common words such as the, of and with, dropped before counting. removed, spaCy's list of 306 words.

Method

Pages with equal cosine rank in node order, in the script and in the search box. A random ranking puts the target first with probability 1 in 303 and in the top five with 5 in 303; a binomial test gives each hit count its p-value.

A miss is labelled from the data, for each model: a short-page win when the winning page has at most a third of the target's words, a missing word when the target shares no content word with the query, and otherwise a win by a page that is not short. The search box runs the stopword-free model on the same vocabulary as the table.

What surprised us

💡What to notice The right page comes first for 1 of 11 queries (p = 0.036 against the 1 in 303 of a random ranking) and in the top five for 6. Removing stopwords lifts that to 2 and 10, so stopwords cost real hits. Page length costs most of the rest: without stopwords, 7 of the 9 misses still lose to a page at most a third as long as the target, because cosine divides by the length of the count vector. With raw counts, 8 of the 10 misses are won by a page shorter than the median page of 1,258 words.

Limitation

We chose the 12 queries and their targets ourselves, so the hit rate says how the search does on our questions, and the single first-place hit, Moon Knight, carries the raw headline. The method also needs the target to exist: the brief's thunder query has no page to find here.

What we checked in the text

Redneck's page wins "weather-controlling mutant from Kenya" while sharing one word with the query besides stopwords, mutant, in its first sentence:

Vincent Stewart, codenamed Redneck and later Skybolt is a fictional mutant character appearing in American comic books published by Marvel Comics.

Redneck (comics)

Its 255 words make that one mutant count for more than all of Storm's 9,831 words, Kenya included.
