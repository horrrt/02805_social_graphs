💡 What to notice The software cluster also holds engineers, accountants and managers: companies hire across the official groups.
Method

We keep certified H-1B filings and identify companies by tax number, as in the other sections. A link counts the companies that filed for both occupations. Filings still on 2010 codes (50 filings) move to their 2018 successors through O*NET's 2010-to-2019 crosswalk. Louvain runs 100 times on the full projection and the best modularity run is kept; the runs agree at a median NMI of 0.99. The null rewires the company × occupation network 20 times, keeping each company's number of occupations and each occupation's number of companies, and projects it again; real and rewired networks are scored on their largest connected piece (z = 362). An occupation's second cluster is the one its employer ties exceed most over the expectation modularity uses (its strength times the cluster's, over twice the total weight). A ratio above 1, our first rule, marks 143 occupations, but the rewired networks mark 448 on average with the same cluster labels. So an occupation now counts only when its ratio beats its own ratio in every rewired network. The disparity filter at α = 0.2, as in the place section, keeps 7,636 of 28,096 links and 344 occupations. Louvain on that backbone finds 7 clusters, which match the full network's at NMI 0.63, against 1.00 between two runs on the full network: the clusters only partly survive the filter. NMI and AMI leave out occupations alone in a cluster and are compared with 100 shuffles of the major-group labels. The same method on 2024 gives clusters that match 2025 at NMI 0.59 on the 386 occupations both years share. Infomap, the random-walk method, finds 9 clusters of two or more occupations; they agree with Louvain's at NMI 0.77 and match the official groups at 0.25.

The shuffled bars keep the clusters fixed and scramble only the official labels. AMI, a version of NMI adjusted for chance agreement, is 0.20. Both scores cover the 433 occupations in clusters of two or more.

Do occupations the same companies hire together also need similar skills?

Yes. Directly co-hired occupations need more alike skills than a random pair of the same 60.

The US Department of Labor's database of what each occupation involves, rated from surveys of workers and analysts. rates every detailed occupation on 109 skills, knowledge areas and work activities. It never looks at which companies file for an occupation, so it checks section 2 with independent data.

💡What to noticeDirectly co-hired pairs average 0.41 The cosine of two occupations' O*NET ratings, from -1 to 1. Higher means more alike skills., above the 0.29 of a random pair from the same 60 occupations.
Method

Every pair here is one of the 60 occupations shown in section 2's jobs network. A direct tie is an edge in that network (the same companies file for both). The random baseline is every pair among those 60, not every occupation O*NET rates, so a large or popular field cannot inflate the answer just by being large.

More numbers

The most alike co-hired pairs: Elementary School Teachers, Except Special Education and Secondary School Teachers, Except Special and Career/Technical Education (0.87); Statisticians and Data Scientists (0.81); General Internal Medicine Physicians and Physicians, All Other (0.81).

The averages cover 158 co-hired pairs and 1770 random pairs, a gap of 0.13. "All Other" codes blend more than one O*NET profile, which can flatten a single pair's similarity toward the average.

Does that agreement hold for whole hiring clusters, not just direct ties?

Mostly. Occupations in the same hiring cluster need more alike skills than pairs in different clusters, even without a direct tie.

Section 2 groups the 60 occupations into hiring clusters with Louvain. This box asks whether those clusters also share skills, leaving out the pairs box 3 already counts.

💡What to noticeSame-cluster pairs without a direct tie average 0.34 similarity, against 0.22 across clusters. Both sit close to the 0.29 random-pair baseline: a hiring cluster tracks skills, but loosely.
Method

Same-cluster pairs exclude the direct ties box 3 already counts, so this box asks a different question: does the cluster as a whole share skills, beyond the companies that directly link two occupations. Different-cluster pairs are every remaining pair across the 60 occupations' cluster boundaries.

The averages cover 734 same-cluster pairs and 878 pairs in different clusters. Section 2 checked its clusters against degree-preserving rewirings.

How do two occupations' day-to-day skills actually compare?

Put up to five H-1B occupations on one radar and see where their O*NET profiles pull apart.

O*NET rates how much each of 109 skills, knowledge areas and work activities matters to an occupation, from 1 (not important) to 5 (extremely important). Each spoke is one of them: further out means it matters more.

Method

A SOC code that names more than one detailed O*NET occupation gets the filing-weighted mean of their ratings, the same weights the O*NET similarity above uses. A rating O*NET flags as too thin or too varied to publish is filled with the mean over every rated occupation. O*NET 31.0 rates no profile for Financial and Investment Analysts (13-2051) or Financial Risk Specialists (13-2054); their ratings come from O*NET 25.0 through O*NET's own 2010-to-2019 crosswalk, the last release that covered them. The ratings come from surveys of people who hold the job and O*NET's own analysts.

Change the damping factor: does the ranking move?

Yes. Raising it from 0.5 to 0.99 moves the ranking away from plain tie counts, toward occupations linked to the biggest hubs.

Each step, a A score from a random walk along the ties: occupations the walk visits often, because well-linked occupations tie to them, score high. walker follows a tie with probability d, the damping factor, or jumps to a random occupation. At d = 0 the ties do not matter; near 1 they decide the order. Pick a value below.

PageRank (bar length)deg #n = that occupation's rank by plain unweighted degree

💡What to noticeThe top 15 at d = 0.5 and at d = 0.99 share only 7 of 15 occupations, so the damping factor reorders the ranking. At d = 0.85 the top 15 shares 10 occupations with the top 15 by degree and 11 with the top 15 by strength: being tied to the right occupations matters as much as how many ties there are.
Method

The network is section 2's companies x occupations projection, kept to its disparity-filter backbone at alpha = 0.05 (stricter than section 2's own alpha = 0.2 backbone, chosen so the ranking has to lean on network position, not just tie count): 289 of the 494 occupations, 2984 of its 28096 ties. A firm-to-client staffing network was tried first and rejected: with no incoming ties for a firm and no outgoing ones for a client, every firm gets the same score and the damping factor cannot reorder the clients either, whatever value it takes. PageRank runs on the occupation network from section 2, trimmed by the disparity filter at α = 0.05 to its largest connected piece.

More numbers

Biggest movers between d = 0.5 and d = 0.99: Physicians, All Other (rank 5 → 45); General Internal Medicine Physicians (rank 29 → 67); Computer Programmers (rank 53 → 22); Network and Computer Systems Administrators (rank 38 → 12).

At d = 0.85, PageRank's top 15 shares 10 of 15 with plain unweighted-degree's top 15, and 11 of 15 with weighted strength's (the projection's own edge weight, shared companies).

Software Developers leads from round 2; the rest of the top 10 settles by round 10.

Every occupation starts with an equal score, and each round passes it along the network's ties: the same walk A score from a random walk along the ties. Here it is computed step by step (power iteration) with damping d = 0.85, and the walk is stopped after each round. repeats until nothing moves. The chart follows the ten occupations that finish on top.

💡What to noticeAfter round 1 Data Scientists lead; from round 2 Software Developers hold first place for good. The grey lines stop crossing by round 10.
Method

The rounds use damping d = 0.85, and the final scores match a standard PageRank calculation within 2e-8.

Each step redistributes (1 - d)/n to every occupation, plus d times the score its ties send it, split by each neighbour's total tie weight. No dangling-node correction is needed: every occupation in this network has at least one tie, unlike a firm-to-client network where one whole side has none.

More numbers

Run to step 60, where every occupation's score matches the standard PageRank routine's fixed point within 2e-8: past that point, one more round would not change the ranking. At round 0 every occupation holds the same score, 1/289, so the chart starts at round 1.
