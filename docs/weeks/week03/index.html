      Is it a power law? We fitted it

      A straight-ish line on log–log axes is not evidence of a power law, and with 228 countries the axis only spans about two decades. So we fitted the tails: maximum likelihood for the exponent, the lower bound chosen by minimising the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance, a goodness-of-fit test against 500 synthetic datasets drawn from the fitted model, and a likelihood-ratio test against the two distributions that also look straight here. The method is Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (2009); the code is analysis/week03_tails.py. The fits are for 2020, the null-model year, and stay there while the charts above follow the slider.

      Tail xmin α In tail Fit p Against the rivals
      Origins per destination 15 1.97 149 0.00 lognormal fits better (p = 0.003)
      People on a corridor 126,665 2.04 377 0.01 lognormal fits better (p = 0.023)
      Destinations per origin 33 2.99 122 0.06 both fit better (p = 0.005, 0.001)
      Flight partners 44 2.81 75 0.09 neither can be separated from it

      Fit p is the share of synthetic datasets from the fitted power law that fit it worse than the real data does. Below 0.1 the power law is ruled out; above it the power law survives, which is not the same as winning. All four are below it. The top two are not close; the bottom two sit at 0.06 and 0.09, near enough to a convention that they are the weakest kind of rejection rather than a verdict. Where a rival can be told apart at all it is the lognormal, and it wins every time it is asked.

      💡 So the word on this page is "heavy-tailed", not "scale-free". Origins per destination fits α = 1.97, which is about the exponent every scale-free network in a lecture slide has, and it is still ruled out at p = 0.00. That is the trap the log–log toggle above sets: a line can look right and be wrong, and the only way to find out is to fit it.

          Roles inside the communities

          Section 11 splits the world into communities. This asks what part each country plays in the one it lands in, on two coordinates from Guimerà and Amaral (Nature 433, 2005): z, how large a country is among the other members of its own community, and P, how evenly its corridors are spread across all of them. A country with P near zero keeps everyone inside one community; near one, it is split evenly between them. The seven names and the cut-offs between them are theirs, drawn on the chart rather than applied out of sight.

          This replaces six labels of our own that compared a 2020 migration ranking against a flight snapshot last refreshed around 2014. Thirty of 228 countries carried a label that mixed the two vintages, and because the flight side cannot move, the section could only ever show one year. Nothing here touches the flight network, so the slider moves it.

          💡
          🧪 Louvain is random, and one run would have animated its own seed. Two seeds at 2020 disagree about 26 of 228 roles, which is the same size as the change from one five-year snapshot to the next. So every year is partitioned 100 times, each run votes, and a country carries the role most runs gave it together with the share that agreed. The coordinates are far steadier than the names: the United States sits at z 5.92 with a standard deviation of 0.03 across the 100 runs, and the wobble is concentrated where a country sits on a threshold. Those are drawn as hollow rings, and they are the ones not to quote.

          What is left after gravity

          Section 4 showed two things separately: rich countries hold more foreign-born people, and the people in them came from further away. Two charts is not a model, so neither one holds the other fixed. Gravity does. It is the standard baseline in the migration literature and it is almost embarrassingly good: people on a corridor rise with the size of both ends and fall with the distance between them.

          Fitted here by Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood on all 8,301 corridors with population and income at both ends, in analysis/week03_gravity.py. That is a few hundred more than the 8,053 in question 5, which needs income at both ends and nothing else; this needs population too, and a handful of places report one without the other. Counts rather than log counts, because logging drops every corridor carrying nobody and biases the rest. Each coefficient is an elasticity: a one per cent rise in the term moves the corridor by that many per cent. Intervals are the heteroskedasticity-robust sandwich the method calls for, which statsmodels fits directly and scikit-learn could not report at all.

          Term Elasticity 95% interval
          Distance between the two−1.19−1.43 to −0.94
          Destination income per head+0.85+0.69 to +1.01
          Destination population+0.69+0.60 to +0.77
          Origin population+0.51+0.43 to +0.59
          Origin income per head−0.25−0.34 to −0.17
          Under 1,000 km apart−0.28−0.78 to +0.22
          💡 Doubling the distance roughly halves the corridor, and that is the single strongest term here. Destination income comes next: with size and distance held fixed, a country twice as rich holds about 1.8 times as many people from any given origin. Origin income runs the other way (poorer origins send more), which is the selection the income bars in section 5 could only show as a marginal. The last row is a failed proxy, kept rather than deleted: this repo has coordinates and no border table, so "share a border" had to be approximated by "centres within 1,000 km", and with distance already in the model it measures nothing. Its interval crosses zero and dropping it moves distance from −1.19 to −1.11 and leaves everything else where it was.

          💡 Read the list and the categories name themselves. Four of the top twelve are Russia and its Soviet successor states, in both directions. Three are the old British settler network: the United Kingdom to New Zealand and Australia, and New Zealand to Australia, which today is a free-movement zone. Two are the ECOWAS free-movement area, Burkina Faso and Ivory Coast exchanging in both directions. Two are wars, Sudan to Chad and Venezuela to Peru. None of these is in the model, and every one of them beats the model by a factor of thirty or more.

          The fit drops every corridor touching a country the World Bank does not price: Syria, South Sudan, Yemen, Eritrea, North Korea and Cuba among others. Those are war zones and sanctioned states, which is to say several of the origins whose corridors would have had the largest residuals. The model is fitted on the part of the world calm enough to be measured, and the missing part is 7.3% of the world's migrants.

          Every term is measured in the same year as the outcome, so nothing here identifies a cause: a corridor thirty times over its prediction is a corridor worth explaining, not an explanation. The fit reaches R² 0.45 across the range of the data and 0.19 on the raw counts, where a handful of enormous corridors dominate.

          The bottom of the residual list, where gravity expects a crowd and DESA reports five people, is a map of which statistics offices file small origins under "other", not of where nobody went.

          Does the world split into groups?

          A course on social graphs asks this in week three and this post had not. Louvain on the undirected network, weighted by people moving in both directions and cut at corridors of 10,000 or more, in analysis/week03_communities.py. It returns eight groups over 205 countries, and they name themselves.

          Group Countries Largest members
          Latin America, Iberia and the western Mediterranean 47 France, Spain, Venezuela, Italy, Colombia
          Germany, Russia and the post-Soviet belt 41 Germany, Russia, Ukraine, Turkey, Syria
          North America and the Pacific rim 35 United States, Mexico, People's Republic of China, Canada, Philippines
          The former British settler network 24 United Kingdom, Australia, South Africa, New Zealand, Zimbabwe
          South Asia and the Gulf 24 India, Saudi Arabia, Bangladesh, Pakistan, United Arab Emirates
          The Horn and central Africa 18 Sudan, South Sudan, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda, Ethiopia

          Afghanistan and Iran 2 Afghanistan, Iran
          💡 70.6% of the world's 272 million migrants live inside one of the eight groups rather than between them, counting every corridor in 2024, including the small ones the 10,000-person threshold left out of the graph. The groups are free movement zones, old empires and shared languages: ECOWAS is one, the Anglosphere settler network is one, the Gulf labour system and the South Asia that staffs it are one, and Afghanistan with Iran is a two-country group held together by forty years of displacement.
          🧪 And the amount of grouping is not a finding. Modularity is 0.533. Rewire the network 100 times keeping every country's number of partners, deal the corridor weights back out at random, and Louvain still returns 0.528 on average. 32 of the 100 shuffles score at or above the real network, which is z = +0.39 and no evidence of anything. Louvain finds communities in noise, so the number on its own was never going to say much. What the shuffles cannot reproduce is which countries land together, and that is where the eight names above come from.

          Why this page weights its betweenness

          The methods note says that betweenness on the raw matrix ranks statistics offices rather than countries. Here is the evidence, from the same script: the same measure, the same year, with a rising floor on how big a corridor has to be to count.

          Corridor floor Corridors left Top brokers
          0 8,795 Australia, Norway, United States, Denmark, Greece
          1,000 3,625 Australia, Canada, France, United States, Italy
          10,000 1,692 Canada, France, United States, Australia, Italy
          50,000 732 France, United States, United Kingdom, Spain, Russia
          100,000 483 United States, France, Germany, United Kingdom, Russia
          💡 At a floor of zero the top brokers are Australia, Norway and Denmark. Raise it and they vanish. None of the three is a hub for anybody. They are register countries: a population register names every origin however small, so they collect hundreds of one-person corridors that a survey-based country files under "other". Those corridors are real rows in the table and they are what put those three on top. By a floor of 100,000 the list is the United States, France, Germany, the United Kingdom and Russia, which is a list of brokers. The measure did not change; what changed is whether a corridor of four people counts as a path.

          Methods, and what this cannot tell you

          Sources, methods and limits

          Migration. UN DESA International Migrant Stock, revision 2024. An arc origin → destination carries the people born in the origin who live in the destination. Subtract two years and you get a change in where people live, not a count of who moved.

          Betweenness. Weighted and directed, distance = 1 / people, so a heavy corridor is a short step. Run it unweighted and the top brokers come out as Australia, Norway and Denmark, which ranks statistics offices: a register names hundreds of tiny origins, a survey buckets them into "other".

          Flights. OpenFlights routes, aggregated from airport pairs to country pairs and counted as distinct routes. One undated snapshot, last refreshed around 2014, so it sits still while the year slider moves. A route existing says nothing about seats or passengers, and week03_passengers.py measures what that costs: against US BTS passenger counts for 2019, route count ranks countries at Spearman 0.87 and misses volume by up to a factor of 41. Degree and betweenness use the same formulas, distance = 1 / routes, so the scatter compares one quantity across two networks.

          Forced displacement. UNHCR Refugee Data Finder, end-2024: refugees plus asylum seekers on the same origin → destination pairs. It answers question 5's second half. The two counts do not nest perfectly (DESA estimates in the middle of 2024, UNHCR counts at the end of it), so on 273 fast-moving corridors, Sudan's most of all, the refugee count exceeds the stock and we cap it there. That makes a crisis corridor read low, never high. A further 2,884 UNHCR pairs, 2.0 million people with Ukraine to the Netherlands among them, have no DESA stock row to attach to and are not on this page at all. Internal displacement is in the source and left out too: these are border crossings.

          Roles. Guimerà and Amaral's cartography (Nature 433, 2005) in analysis/week03_cartography.py, on the same graph section 11 partitions: undirected, weighted by people both ways, corridors under 10,000 people dropped. Two coordinates per country, both computed on strength rather than on a count of partners, because a corridor here runs from a dozen people to four million. Louvain is stochastic, so each year is partitioned 100 times, every run votes for a role, and a country carries the role most runs gave it. The coordinates printed beside a role are averaged over the runs that produced that role and not over all of them: pooled, four countries came out with a participation coefficient on the wrong side of their own name. The thresholds are Guimerà and Amaral's own and are not tuned to this network, which is much denser than the ones they were set on, so peripheral stays the largest group. Tuning them to balance our own chart would be seven arbitrary numbers, which is what this replaced.

          What the roles replaced. Six labels of our own, from a cascade of rank tests, three of which compared a 2020 migration ranking against the undated flight snapshot. Thirty of 228 countries carried one, the section could only ever show a single year, and 141 of 228 came out "mixed". Better flight data would have fixed it and there is none: ICAO sells a dated global country-pair table, Sabre licenses one, Eurostat publishes a free dated one that only Europe reports into, and OpenSky is free, dated and CC BY but its coverage follows the ADS-B receivers, which sit in Europe and North America. Each trades a vintage problem for a coverage problem, so the roles now use no flight data at all.

          Heavy tails. The Clauset, Shalizi and Newman procedure in analysis/week03_tails.py: maximum likelihood for the exponent, the lower bound by minimising the KS distance, 500 synthetic datasets for the goodness-of-fit p, and Vuong likelihood-ratio tests against lognormal and exponential on the same tail. Both rivals are fitted with the continuous approximation, and so is the power law inside the comparison, because a ratio between a probability and a probability density means nothing. Full output in analysis/week03_tails.json.

          Why these four numbers changed. The fitting used to be our own code and is now the powerlaw package, which is the reference implementation of that procedure, and the swap found a bug we had been publishing. Our KS distance walked the sorted tail one observation at a time, so a degree value appearing ten times produced ten steps in the empirical curve against one flat stretch of the model, and that gap counted as distance. Degree sequences are nothing but ties: 227 countries share 88 distinct in-degrees. The exponent was never wrong, and corridor weights, which barely tie, were right to the digit. But the inflated distance moved the xmin that minimises it, so all three degree tails had been fitted above the wrong cut. Destinations per origin is the one that changes its mind: it used to read p = 0.88, a power law surviving comfortably, and it reads 0.06.

          Which countries, and how many. Six different counts appear on this page and all of them are right, so here they are in one place. 236 countries and territories are in the payload, the union of the migration and flight networks. 228 have migration figures and sit on at least one corridor. 208 have a flight route. 212 have any World Bank indicator and 197 have income per head, which is why the wealth charts say 173 once population and a positive foreign-born share are also required. 205 survive the 10,000-person floor in the community graph and so carry a role in section 6, where 193 did in 1990. 185 report to the Oxford tracker, and 87 have both a flight route to the United States and passengers on it.

          The countries without a price. Of the 39 with no income per head, the list is not random: Syria, South Sudan, Yemen, Eritrea, North Korea and Cuba, alongside two dozen small territories. Those are war zones, sanctioned states and dependencies, and the war zones are among the largest refugee origins on the page. Every income-based result here therefore excludes them: the gravity model, the wealth scatter and the income tiers all drop corridors touching a country the World Bank does not price, which is 7.3% of the world's migrants. It biases those results toward the parts of the world that are calm enough to be measured.

          Reporting coverage. DESA's table is ragged. The United States names 58 origins in every year of the table and Denmark names 190, because one runs a survey with an "other" bucket and the other keeps a population register. Every partner count on this page inherits that gap.

          Monthly applications. Eurostat migr_asyappctzm, first-time asylum applicants of all ages and both sexes, by citizenship and reporting country, monthly since 2013. Reuse with attribution. Eurostat ships aggregate rows inside both dimensions (EU27 sits beside its own members, TOTAL beside every citizenship), and summing over them counts most of Europe twice, so both are dropped. Applications are not arrivals, temporary protection is a different route and is not counted, and small cells are suppressed, which is why a month with no figure is drawn as a hole rather than a zero.

          Border closures. Oxford Covid-19 Government Response Tracker, CC BY 4.0, indicator C8: policy toward foreign travellers on a 0 to 4 scale, one value per country per day, national rows only. Level 4 is a ban on all regions or a total closure. It is policy on paper rather than traffic, the panel is 185 countries and the number reporting moves from day to day, and the tracker stops at the end of 2022.

          The deaths calendar. IOM Missing Migrants Project via HDX, CC BY-IGO, binned by the date an incident was reported. It is one of the three dated sources here, with Eurostat by the month and the Oxford tracker by the day, and the only one that is global and dated at once. It counts recorded incidents: IOM says the record is an undercount by an unknown amount that differs by route, so a brighter year can be a better-watched one rather than a worse one. A single cell is frequently a single boat, and the number missing after a shipwreck is estimated from what survivors say.

          The map. Natural Earth 1:110m admin-0 boundaries, public domain. scripts/migration/build_world_outline.py keeps the ISO code and the name, rounds coordinates to two decimals and gets the file to 169 KB. Borders are here so you can see where a corridor lands, and claim nothing about any dispute.

          Render variants. Six renderers and eleven style dimensions over one dataset. Only the drawing changes: a variant overrides the visuals it improves and inherits the rest. The libraries sit in the repository, so the page fetches no third-party JavaScript. Section 10 is the one exception to the renderer choice: its three views are ECharts whichever renderer you picked, and the megabyte that costs is fetched when you open the section and not before.

          The basemap. NASA Blue Marble, public domain, offered in every renderer. On the 2D canvas we resample it through the orthographic projection at two thirds of the drawn size and scale it up, which keeps a drag smooth at no visible cost.

          Statistics. Every interval on this page is computed rather than assumed. Correlations carry a Fisher interval; the weighted median of distance carries a percentile interval over 400 resamples of the corridors, resampled by corridor because a corridor is the thing that was observed. The null model runs 100 shuffles, so the finest p it can express is one in a hundred and a z above about 2.5 is a floor rather than a measurement. Seven invariants across the five data files run in tests/week03-data.test.mjs: totals agreeing between the two migration files, no aggregate codes leaking into a country list, and the forced-displacement cap still covering the number of corridors this page says it does.

          Rebuilding this. scripts/rebuild_week03.py downloads the sources and recreates every file the page loads; --check lists what is committed and what made it. The raw inputs stay out of the repository. A style guide draws every component under every skin.

          AI use. We built the harvest, the analysis and this page with Claude. Every number on the page comes out of a script in analysis/: week03_corridor_control.py for the networks, then _tails, _gravity, _communities, _country_networks, _asylum, _closures and _calendar. None is typed by hand.
