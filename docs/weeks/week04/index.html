Opening

what the filings show

An H-1B filing is an employer’s request to hire a non-US worker in a specialty occupation. It names the job, the worksite, the wage, and the company asking for permission to employ them. The Department of Labor records the filing even when the worker never arrives, changes employer, or is ultimately not hired.

Why start with companies? A job title tells us what work is requested; a company tells us which jobs, places, and clients are connected by the same hiring system. That makes the employer the thread linking the three networks in this story. The network is about shared filings, not friendships between workers or companies.

! Read the scope carefully These are visa-sponsored filings, not all hiring in America. They leave out workers without H-1B sponsorship, employers that never file, rejected or withdrawn applications, and the wider conditions that shape who gets hired.

Unless stated otherwise, the figures below use certified H-1B filings in FY2025. One filing is a request, not a guaranteed job.

Where the hiring is

(companies × cities · FY2025)

Cities group by who hires there, not by region, and no single link holds the map together. The cities are the 40 metro areas with the most filings, 84.5% of the year's total. Two metros are linked when the same company files in both; the link weighs, summed over those companies, the smaller of the company's two filing counts. Louvain splits the 40 metros into three groups: seven large hubs led by New York and Dallas, eight tech hubs led by San Jose and San Francisco, and the other 25. The split is weak but real: modularity 0.049 against 0.013 for rewired networks in which each company keeps its number of metros (z = 33). The two questions below ask what the groups follow and where the network comes apart.

Do cities group by who hires there instead of by region?

(communities vs four labellings)

By who hires. The groups follow how much of a city's hiring runs through consulting and IT-services firms better than they follow Census regions. We gave each metro four labels: its Census region, its Census division, the third it falls in by the share of its filings that place a worker at a client, and the third it falls in by the share filed by professional and technical services firms (NAICS 54, the sector of IT consultancies). Thirds, because 35 of the 40 metros have that sector as their largest, so "largest sector" says almost nothing. Each bar is the adjusted mutual information (AMI) between the Louvain groups and one labelling: 0 means no better than labels dealt at random, 1 means the same grouping. AMI corrects for the number of labels, so four regions and three thirds compare fairly.

How well each labelling matches the Louvain groups

AMI for each labelling of the 40 metros; the p-value is the share of 1,000 shuffles of that labelling that match at least as well. Orange bars describe who hires, grey bars where the city is.

💡 What to notice The IT-services share matches the groups at AMI 0.17 (p = 0.002) and the placed share at 0.12 (p = 0.012). Census regions reach 0.06 (p = 0.11) and divisions 0.06 (p = 0.08), no better than chance. Seven of the eight tech-hub metros sit in the lowest third by placed share: there, companies mostly hire for themselves. In the New York–Dallas group the median metro places 27% of its filings at a client and files 60% through IT-services firms. The match is modest: most of what makes two metros alike stays unexplained.
B

Where does the backbone break, and whose links hold it?

(disparity filter, one link at a time)

Nowhere in one place. The map sheds metros one or two at a time, and the big outsourcing firms hold no more of those links than of any others. Every pair of the 40 metros shares some employer, so the full network is one hairball of 780 links. The disparity filter keeps a link when it carries an unusually large share of either metro's total weight; α is the test's threshold, and a smaller α keeps fewer links. Tried at five values, the largest piece of the map fell from 32 metros at α = 0.1 to 19 at α = 0.05, which looks like one snap. So we removed the links one by one, least significant first, and watched the largest piece after each removal.

Metros in the largest piece as the filter tightens

Each step is one or more links removed. Read right to left: as α falls, links go and metros drop out of the largest connected piece. The shaded band is the range from α = 0.1 to 0.05 where the five-value sweep saw the drop.

💡 What to notice The first metro falls off at α = 0.135. No single removal cuts off more than two metros (Chicago–Louisville at α = 0.041, New York–Detroit at 0.022), and the fall from 32 to 19 is 18 separate links, each peeling one metro away. The company adding most weight to those links is often a direct employer: Amazon leads four, Citigroup two, JPMorgan Chase, FedEx, Capital One and Intel one each. Of the 20 links whose removal cuts a metro loose, the five largest placing firms (Tata Consultancy Services, Cognizant, Infosys, HCL, Compunnel) lead 6 (30%), against 70 of the 180 links in the whole backbone at α = 0.2 (39%, p = 0.27).

Which jobs go together

companies × occupations · FY2025

Employers reveal bundles of work. Two occupations are linked when the same companies file for both. Clusters come from the whole network of … occupations, and they are real: modularity … against … for rewired networks in which every company keeps its number of occupations. Software developers sit in almost every company's mix, so most links run through them. The two questions below test the clusters from two sides: do outsourcing firms and direct employers bundle jobs the same way, and does any job belong to two bundles at once?

A

Do outsourcing firms bundle jobs differently from direct employers?

two networks, one comparison

Mostly not. Their job clusters differ about as much as two random groups of companies of the same sizes. We split the companies in two: the 818 firms that place 20 or more filings at client sites (21% of all filings) and the 58,412 others. Each group gets its own occupation network and its own Louvain clusters, and the normalized mutual information (NMI) says how alike the two clusterings are on the 217 occupations that sit in a cluster of two or more on both sides. Alone, that number means little: splitting companies into a small and a large group changes the clusters even if nobody behaves differently. So the baseline splits the companies at random, 20 times.

Observed agreement against random splits

NMI between the two groups' clusters. Whiskers span one standard deviation over 20 random splits. One baseline keeps the number of companies in each group, the other the number of filings.

The largest occupations in each group

Share of each group's filings, for the eight occupations with the most filings overall.

💡 What to notice The two clusterings agree at NMI 0.42. Random groups with the same numbers of companies agree at 0.47 ± 0.07, so the real split is inside the noise. Random groups with the same filing volumes agree at 0.58 ± 0.05; they hold many more companies, which is why we read the first baseline as the fair one. Software developers are 28% of the outsourcing firms' filings and 33% of the direct employers'. The difference is breadth: direct employers file for 283 occupations the outsourcing firms never touch.
B

Does any job belong to two clusters at once?

link communities

Not clearly. On a network this dense, link communities pour almost everything into one cluster. A Louvain partition puts every job in exactly one cluster. Link communities (Ahn, Bagrow and Lehmann, 2010) group the links instead, so a job belongs to every community its links are in. The cut is chosen where partition density D, the average of how close each community is to a complete one, peaks. On the 28,155 links between 501 occupations it peaks at D = 0.57 with one community holding 85% of the links and 121 small ones of three links or more beside it.

💡 What to notice A job's number of communities mostly counts its links (Spearman 0.84), so the table ranks by communities per link, as the course suggests. The top is small occupations such as physical therapist aides (4 communities over 10 links). Only 2 of the 7 occupations that section 2's first test flagged as bridges appear in it: credit counselors and interviewers. Two methods give two different lists of small occupations, so we cannot name a job that clearly sits in two clusters.

Who staffs whom

firm → client · FY2025

One certified H-1B filing in five names a client company as the worksite. The Louvain groups of firms and clients are weak, but clients that change vendor stay inside them. Here the network links an outsourcing firm to each client company where it places workers, and a link weighs the filings between them. Louvain runs on the largest connected piece: 21,759 firms and clients, 41,212 links. Counted once per link, the groups beat rewired networks in which every firm and client keeps its number of partners (modularity 0.57 against 0.53), and they follow the client's industry about as much as its main vendor. The three questions below ask whether the groups behave like markets.

A

When a client changes its main vendor, does it stay in its group?

FY2022 to FY2025

Yes, eight times as often as chance: one switch in four stays inside the client's group. A client's main vendor is the firm that files most placements there in a year. We followed the clients with five or more filings in two consecutive years and found 1,271 switches of main vendor between FY2022 and FY2025. For each, we asked whether the new vendor sat in the client's Louvain group of the earlier year. The baseline draws a new vendor at random, a large firm as often as its filings make it likely.

Switches that stay inside the client's group

Share of switches whose new main vendor is in the client's group of the earlier year, for each pair of years. Grey bars are the baseline's mean over 1,000 draws; whiskers span one standard deviation.

💡 What to notice Pooled over the three pairs of years, 25.8% of switches stay in the group against 3.4% ± 0.5% for random vendors (z = 47). Part of that is familiarity: 61% of new main vendors already placed someone at the client the year before. A stricter baseline that draws only among those firms still falls short each year (24% against 19%, 27% against 20%, 28% against 24%). The groups hold real information about where a client turns next.
B

Which clients change group when filing counts are ignored?

weighted vs unweighted

Two in three, but much of that is Louvain's own noise, and the movers are not mainly the clients with several vendors. We ran Louvain with links weighted by filings and with every link counting one, matched each weighted group to the unweighted group it overlaps most, and called a client a mover when its matched group changed. Two runs of the same kind with different seeds set the noise floor.

💡 What to notice 65.7% of clients move between the weighted and unweighted partitions, above the 34.6% between two weighted seeds and the 52.3% between two unweighted ones. We expected the movers to be clients with several vendors, since only their filing counts can pull them one way or another. They are, but barely: 31.4% of movers have two or more vendors, against 26.9% of all clients. The largest movers are the largest clients: Citigroup sits with Tata Consultancy Services when filings count and with EXL when they do not; Bank of America moves from Randstad's group to Tata Consultancy Services'.

Which clients sit in two groups at once?

split loyalties

1,842 clients get a fifth or more of their filings from a second group, fewer than in rewired networks. A Louvain partition gives each client one group, but a client with several vendors can draw on firms from different groups. We counted clients whose second group supplies at least 20% of their filings, and did the same on 100 rewired networks in which every firm and client keeps its number of partners.

Clients split between two groups

The count for the real network against the mean of 100 rewired networks; the whisker spans one standard deviation.

💡 What to notice The rewired networks give 2,041 ± 28 split clients, so the real groups hold their clients together more than chance does (z = −7). A caution: rewiring breaks the network into smaller groups, which split clients more easily, so the gap is partly a difference in group size. The largest split clients are banks, insurers and manufacturers. USAA gets 44% of its filings from HCL's group and 26% from Tata Consultancy Services'; Stellantis 43% from L&T Technology Services' group and 39% from Tata Consultancy Services'.

Without the biggest firms

metros and jobs · FY2025

Take out the ten largest filers and the metro map turns regional; the job clusters mostly hold. A handful of companies file a large share of everything: the five largest placing firms (Tata Consultancy Services, Cognizant, Infosys, HCL, Compunnel) file 5.6% of the filings in the 40 metros, and the ten largest filers of any kind 18.8%. A company that files everywhere links every pair of metros and every pair of its jobs, so its footprint could be all the structure there is. We removed each set, reran 100 Louvain runs on the metro network and on the job network (weighted here by filings, since a count of companies barely moves when ten of 59,230 leave), and compared the groups with the full network's. Removing less data changes the groups too, so each removal sits beside 20 random cuts of companies that remove the same share of filings.

How much do the groups change?

NMI between the groups after a removal and the full network's groups: 1 means unchanged. Metros on the left, jobs on the right.

💡 What to notice Without the ten largest filers (Amazon, Cognizant, Google, Microsoft, EY, Meta, Apple, Tata Consultancy Services, Deloitte, Infosys), the metro groups match Census regions at AMI 0.16 (p = 0.007), against 0.06 for the full network and 0.01 ± 0.03 for random cuts. The national employers are what hide the regional pattern. Removing the five placing firms instead moves the metros to a two-group split at NMI 0.64, the value of the other split Louvain already finds in 36 of 100 runs of the full network, so that change says more about the split's instability than about the firms. The job clusters hold at NMI 0.90 and 0.81, though random cuts leave them closer still (0.95 and 0.89); they also sharpen, with modularity rising from 0.28 to 0.32 without the ten largest filers. Every version still beats its own rewired networks by a wide margin (metros z = 23 or more).

Beyond the three networks

law firms · green cards · wage levels · FY2025

The section 3 groups barely show in lawyers or green cards; the outsourcing firms stand out in the wage levels they file. The same filings name the law firm that prepared them, and the Department of Labor's green-card files name the employers that sponsor permanent residence. Three short questions follow the section 3 groups into those records.

A

Do immigration law firms split companies the way vendors do?

employer × law firm

Barely. Law firms group the companies along the section 3 lines only slightly more than chance. 75.6% of certified filings name a law firm; after name cleaning there are 4,479. We linked each firm that places workers in section 3's network to its law firms, weighted by filings, ran Louvain, and compared the groups with the section 3 groups for the 4,576 firms in both. Most companies use one law firm: 64% of filings come from such companies, which makes the network a set of stars.

Agreement with the section 3 groups

AMI between the law-firm groups and the staffing groups, against 50 rewired law-firm networks in which every company and law firm keeps its number of partners.

💡 What to notice AMI 0.037 against 0.000 ± 0.002 for rewired networks: real (z = 18) and small. Modularity would mislead here. The law-firm network scores 0.90, below the 0.93 of its rewired copies, because stars split into near-perfect groups whatever the wiring. Fragomen files the most (14,087 filings for 131 of these companies); EY Law files 10,274 for eight.

Fewer per H-1B filing, but the staffing groups do not differ beyond chance. A green card through work starts with a PERM filing. Here the outsourcing firms are the 818 companies that place 20 or more filings at client sites. C

Do outsourcing firms file at lower wage levels for the same job?

prevailing-wage levels I to IV

Yes. For the same occupation, a filing that places a worker at a client has 3.6 times the odds of sitting at wage level I or II. Every filing states a prevailing-wage level from I (entry) to IV (fully competent), set by the experience and skills the job asks for; each level carries a wage floor. 92% of filings give one. We compared placed and direct filings within each occupation, so software developers are compared with software developers.

Share of filings at level I or II

The five occupations with the most filings. Orange bars are filings that place the worker at a client, blue bars filings for the employer's own site.

💡 What to notice Overall, 79% of placed filings sit at level I or II against 58% of direct ones. Within the 83 occupations with 20 or more filings of each kind, the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio is 3.59 (95% interval 3.53 to 3.66), and 69 of the 83 point the same way. Software developers: 87% against 52%. Offered wages follow: placed filings offer a median 1.00 times the prevailing wage in the five largest occupations, direct ones 1.02 to 1.08 times. A level describes the job as filed, not the worker, so this shows cheaper job descriptions, not lower pay for the same person.

Closing

one reading of the network

The groups in these networks are weak, but they are not noise. Cities group by who hires there, not by region. Outsourcing firms and direct employers bundle jobs alike. And when a client drops its main vendor, the new one comes from the same Louvain group eight times as often as chance. Take out the ten largest filers, most of them national tech and consulting employers, and the metro groups start to follow Census regions.

Where outsourcing shows most is outside the networks: a filing that places a worker at a client has 3.6 times the odds of a lower wage level for the same occupation. Lawyers and green cards barely follow the staffing groups.

What surprised us. The staffing groups looked weak in our first round, following industry about as much as vendor, yet 25.8% of vendor switches stay inside them, against 3.4% for a random vendor. And the backbone that seemed to snap between α = 0.1 and 0.05 never snaps: no single link cuts off more than two metros.

! One important limit A shared employer link means that the same companies file for both occupations or in both places. It does not prove that the jobs are performed together, that one caused the other, or that the network represents workers who were actually hired.

How the comparison works

We keep certified H-1B filings and identify companies by tax number, as in the other sections. A link counts the companies that filed for both occupations. Filings still on 2010 computer codes (…) move to their 2018 successors. Louvain runs 100 times on the full projection and the best modularity run is kept; the runs agree at a median NMI of …. The null rewires the company × occupation network … times, keeping each company's number of occupations and each occupation's number of companies, and projects it again; real and rewired networks are scored on their largest connected piece (z = …). An occupation's second cluster is the one its employer ties exceed most over the expectation modularity uses (its strength times the cluster's, over twice the total weight). A ratio above 1, our first rule, marks … occupations, but the rewired networks mark … on average with the same cluster labels. So an occupation now counts only when its ratio beats its own ratio in every rewired network. The disparity filter at α = …, as in the place section, keeps … of … links and … occupations. Louvain on that backbone finds … clusters, which match the full network's at NMI …, against … between two runs on the full network: the clusters only partly survive the filter. NMI and AMI leave out occupations alone in a cluster and are compared with 100 shuffles of the major-group labels. The same method on FY2024 gives clusters that match FY2025 at NMI … on the … occupations both years share. Infomap, the random-walk method, finds … clusters of two or more occupations; they agree with Louvain's at NMI … and match the official groups at ….

Who staffs whom: four earlier questions

How many workers sit at a client?

A filing is a request to employ someone, not a hire. In FY2025, 104,732 of the 537,796 certified filings (19.5%) mark a client site. The worksites file lists every client a filing names; we leave out the 16% of client entries that name no company, such as "Home Address", and the 1,881 where a firm names itself. Counted that way, 101,763 filings (18.9%) name a client company: the worker is employed by one company and works at another, down from 21.9% in FY2022. Placing firms also fare worse at USCIS: in FY2022 it denied 2.7% of their first-time petitions, against 1.3% for direct employers.

Do clients group by industry or by the firm that staffs them?

By both, weakly. With every firm–client link counted once, Louvain splits the network into about 65 groups, and the split beats rewired networks that keep everyone's number of partners (modularity 0.57 against 0.53). Among the 862 clients with a known industry and two or more firms, the groups match the main vendor at an adjusted mutual information (AMI) of 0.09 and the industry at 0.07; both beat shuffled labels. AMI corrects NMI for chance, which matters here: these clients have 339 main vendors but only 18 industries. Counting filings pulls clients to their main vendor (AMI 0.51 against 0.06), but that split scores below rewired networks with the same filing counts (0.60 against 0.74), and the vendor's head start is built in: the vendor is a node in the same network, and 87% of these clients land in its group.

Who relies on a single vendor?

Small clients. 14,679 of the 18,924 clients use one firm, but they hold 22% of placed filings. Of the 629 clients with 20 or more filings, 69 get over 90% from one firm, and the median one gets 37% from its largest. Citigroup, the largest client, uses 114 firms, and Tata Consultancy Services supplies a quarter. The eight largest firms supply only 27% of what the 20 largest clients receive.

Does it hold from year to year?

Only in part. On the clients present in both years, consecutive years agree at NMI 0.21 to 0.27, about half the 0.47 to 0.52 between two runs of the same year on the same clients. We compared vendor and industry on FY2025 only.
