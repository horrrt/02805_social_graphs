The Marvel network gets language

Headline question and the one figure that answers it. Pick the strongest of the seven sections once the results are in.

Opening

What the data is: 303 English Wikipedia pages from Category:Marvel Comics superheroes, frozen 26 August 2026, and the links between them. What a link means, what a token is, and which question the post answers. Self-contained: no earlier post needed.

1

Turn links into relationships

Links written in fight words reach across the network's communities, and links written in family words stay inside them.

What we asked

Do enemies sit in different communities from allies and family?

Mostly yes: links described with fight words cross between communities more often than chance, and links described with family words less often. The word list is right about half the time, so read both as tendencies.

What we did

A link from A to B means A's Wikipedia page links to B's. For each of the 1,784 links we took the first sentence on A's page that names B, and found one for 1,513 (85%). Most of the rest come from infoboxes and navigation boxes, which our copy of the text has lost; 33 point at one of 7 alternate versions, such as the film Jean Grey, whose name belongs to the main character.

A small word list labels each sentence: killed, family, enemy, ally or teammate. It matches lower-case words only, so a name such as Doctor Nemesis supplies no label. 836 of the sentences (55%) match no word at all.

The communities come from 100 Louvain runs on the link network. For each label we counted how often its links join two different communities, averaged over the runs, and compared it with 1,000 shuffles of the labels over the 677 labelled links.

Method

Each page splits into paragraphs at its line breaks and into sentences with spaCy's rule-based sentencizer. Sentences under References, External links, Notes, See also and Further reading are dropped. A character is found by its page title without the disambiguation ("Storm (Marvel Comics)" is "Storm") and by the real name its description gives in brackets ("Emil Blonsky" for the Abomination), as a whole word. A name several pages go by belongs to the main page: "Spider-Man" is the main Spider-Man, not the Mangaverse one.

A sentence that matches several labels takes the first of killed, family, enemy, ally, teammate. Negation is not handled. Louvain runs on the weighted, undirected link network with seeds 2805 to 2904. The runs disagree with each other, so the test uses how often each link crosses over all 100 runs, for the real labels and the shuffled ones alike.

More numbers

A stricter shuffle only swaps labels between links that leave the same page, so a page written in hostile words throughout cannot carry the result. Enemies still cross more than it predicts (46%, z = 3.6) and family less (33%, z = −3.7). A pair of characters who link both ways counts twice above; counting each pair once gives z = 3.6 for enemies and −4.0 for family.

The labelled links: 217 teammate, 202 enemy, 116 family, 84 killed and 58 ally. 113 sentences match more than one label.

Figure

Links that join two communities, by label

Dot: how often that label's links join two different communities, averaged over 100 Louvain runs. Band: the same share when the labels are shuffled, mean and one standard deviation either side.

What surprised us

💡What to notice 54% of enemy links join two communities, against 42% when the labels are shuffled (z = 4.3). Family links cross only 23% of the time (z = −4.6). Allies cross 31%, still within chance; teammate and killed links look like shuffled ones.

Limitation

The word list reads the sentence, not the pair. Of the 60 labels we read, 32 describe how A and B relate. A long sentence that names B often tells of a third character: on Cyclops's page, "Jean … is killed shortly after in battle with Xorn". Ally labels hold up best (9 of 12), enemy and killed worst (5 of 12 each), family in between (6 of 12). So "enemy" means a sentence with fight words, and it also fits "Phyla then goes with Nova and Star-Lord to lead a final battle against Annihilus".

What we checked in the text

The 60 sentences we read, 12 per label, drawn at random. ✓ means the label describes how A and B relate; ✗ says what the sentence is about instead.

Catch Wikipedia copying itself

Pages copy each other when their characters share a codename or a team, and what they copy is mostly publication history and lists of films and games, rarely the character's story.

What we asked

Which Marvel pages copy text from each other?

Pages about characters who share a codename or a team: 22 pairs in 12 clusters, and 20 of those pairs already link to each other.

What we did

We split every page into lower-case words, 740,282 in all, and collected every run of eight words in a row, an 8-gram. A run found on many pages is house style, not copying: "in American comic books published by Marvel Comics" is on 282 of the 303 pages. So 8-grams on more than 10 pages are set aside.

The remaining 8-grams merge into passages, overlapping runs counting once. Two pages copy each other when they share a passage of 30 words or more. The copying network links those pages, and its connected pieces are the clusters.

Method

A word is a run of letters and digits, with an apostrophe inside a word kept ("jean's"); punctuation and line breaks are dropped. Each word keeps its place on the page, so every shared passage is quoted from the page itself, and a passage copied twice on a page counts twice. A passage is labelled with the section heading above it on each of the two pages. What ties each cluster's characters was read from their pages.

The check: a pair that copies is compared with all 45,753 pairs of pages and with the 2,830 pairs that share only a phrase, an 8-gram run shorter than 30 words. A link counts in either direction.

What surprised us

💡What to notice 20 of the 22 copying pairs link to each other, against 3.1% of all pairs of pages and 16% of pairs that share only a phrase. The two that do not are Wild Child's: his page shares a stock sentence about Krakoa with Storm's and Rachel Summers's. Five clusters share a codename (Venom and Eddie Brock), six a team (Rocket Raccoon and Star-Lord) and one a family. Counted on both pages of each pair, 35% of the copied words sit under Publication history and 24% under In other media, the lists of films and games, and only 5% in the character's biography.

Limitation

The text has no edit history, so we cannot tell who copied whom. The house-style cutoff barely matters: any cutoff from 3 to 20 pages gives the same 22 pairs. The passage length does: at 20 words, 80 pairs copy and 46 of them link; at 50 words, 11 copy and all of them link. And runs of 12 words add 16 pairs from a templated lead that 8-word runs split, on eight Strikeforce: Morituri pages whose leads come in two versions.

What we checked in the text

The longest passage of the three largest copying pairs, and the templated lead the 12-word runs find, quoted from the first page of each pair.

A Marvel search engine in 20 lines

Can bag-of-words find the right page from a description?

What we asked

If we treat a short description such as “Norse god of thunder” as a tiny document, turn every Wikipedia page into a Bag-of-Words vector, and rank the 303 pages by cosine similarity — how often does the right character come first?

What we did

Lowercased alphanumeric tokens, CountVectorizer over all 303 pages, cosine similarity on raw counts. Twelve fixed queries with a target page each. Baseline: a uniform guess among 303 pages (~0.33%). We also reran the same queries with English stopwords removed, to see how much of/with/a were steering the ranking. Script: analysis/week05_search.py.

      Heaps' law of the Marvel universe

      Do minor characters bring new words, or repeat the famous ones?

      Heaps' law describes a familiar pattern in text: as we read more words, the number of different words keeps rising, but usually more slowly. Here the reading order comes from the network, so we can ask whether the Marvel pages that receive the most incoming links introduce the vocabulary that shapes the corpus.

      What we asked

      When pages are added from highest in-degree to lowest, does the vocabulary grow differently than it does in the opposite order? Do less-linked pages add genuinely new words, or mostly more names and repeated Marvel language?

      What we did

      We lowercased words, kept internal apostrophes and hyphens, and counted tokens and distinct word types as pages entered the corpus. We compared the most-linked-first curve with the reverse order and 200 random page orders. The pages and text stayed fixed; only the order changed.

      What surprised us

      After 10 pages, the most-linked order has 9,800 distinct types, against 1,963 in the reverse order and 4,404 ± 976 in random orders. By page 303 every order converges to the same 29,407 types. The network changes which vocabulary arrives first, not the final vocabulary.

      What we checked in the text

      One low-in-degree page, Helix, adds a specialised plot sentence: “The Jackal experiments on Rafael, mutating him further.” It also carries names and recurring article language, so a new type is not automatically a new idea.

      Limitation

      Incoming links measure connections in this Wikipedia snapshot, not importance in every Marvel story. The most-linked order also starts with much more text, so its early vocabulary lead mixes page order with page length.

      6

      Does network fame buy you more words?

      Do more-linked characters get longer pages?

      A page can be famous in the network and still have little to say in the snapshot, or be long because its biography has accumulated many details. This section separates the number of incoming links from the amount of text attached to a character.

      What we asked

      Do characters with more incoming links have longer Wikipedia pages? Which pages sit far above or below the overall relationship, and what does reading those pages tell us about the gap?

      What we did

      For all 303 pages, including the 58 pages with zero in-degree in this snapshot, we compare token count with in-degree. A log-scaled comparison makes short and long pages visible together. We inspect pages furthest above or below the fitted relationship.

      Figure

      What surprised us

      Connection and page length travel together: Pearson correlation is 0.77 between log1p(in-degree) and log(token count). But Quasar has 24 incoming links and only 1,018 tokens, while Brian Braddock has one incoming link and 7,226 tokens.

      What we checked in the text

      Brian Braddock’s opening calls him “the champion of the British Isles and its peoples” and follows several publication histories. Quasar’s page opens with several heroes sharing the name and then starts Wendell Vaughn’s biography.

      Limitation

      In-degree is a property of this link network, not a direct measure of cultural fame. Page length reflects editing history, plot detail, and article conventions, so the correlation describes this snapshot rather than proving that fame causes more words.

      7

      Who has the weirdest Wikipedia page?

      Which Marvel page is the weirdest, and is it really?

      “Weird” needs a rule before it can become a ranking. A page may look unusual because its character is unusual, or because the text contains a template, a list of names, or a formatting habit that the corpus happens to preserve.

      What we asked

      Which of the 303 Marvel pages is most unusual under a definition built from this week’s language tools, and does reading the page show genuinely strange content or only strange-looking formatting and boilerplate?

      What we did

      We define weirdness as the residual from a linear fit of type-token ratio against log page length. A positive score means a page has more distinct word types than pages of similar length. We rank the pages, then read the highest-ranked articles by hand.

      What surprised us

      The top page is Coldblood, with 381 types in 667 tokens and a score of +0.093. The ranking is a prompt for inspection rather than a verdict: it finds unusually varied vocabulary, not characters with objectively strange lives.

      What we checked in the text

      The winner begins, “Coldblood-7 (Eric Savin) is a fictional character appearing in American comic books published by Marvel Comics.” The score can come from proper names and a compact biography, so it found lexical variety rather than an objective kind of weirdness.

      Limitation

      There is no objective universal score for a weird Wikipedia page. Rare language can reflect a proper name, a niche story, or a copy-editing artifact, and a length correction cannot remove every effect of article structure.
